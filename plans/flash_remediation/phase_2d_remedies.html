Flash remediation — Phase 2d: recommended remedies

The proposed fixes for axial truncation, lateral truncation, and the split-reconstruction seam — rationale, evidence, implementation sketches, and an honest weighing of pros and cons (including "do nothing") · 2026-07-09 · evidence pages: phase_1_results.html, phase_2a_axial_results.html, phase_2b_radial_results.html, phase_2c_split_results.html · plan of record: flash_remediation_plan.md · Originally written plan-only; implementation began 2026-07-11 on greg/kernel_investigation (axial first, real-data validation after each step) — this page is the design record.

TL;DR — the three verdicts.

  • Axial: extend the slab to the exact geometric bound automatically, inside auto_set_recon_geometry — a separate excess for each end (they differ under det_row_offset, and helical z-shifts attach per end), with R taken from the recon grid. Truncation is the norm in practice — a holder or fixture virtually always leaves the field of view at one end — so an always-on extension is used on at least one end, over-padding is provably harmless, and no threshold exists at all; the waste is bounded by one end's share of ~10–20% more slices.
  • Lateral: detect and warn, but do not auto-pad — the required padding ("cover the object") is not derivable from geometry or reliably from truncated data, under-padding costs orders of magnitude, and lateral padding is O(scale²) in memory: this is the finicky-threshold case where assisted-manual beats automation.
  • Split seam: set hrecon from the geometry formula as the default (threshold-free, +~3% memory, verified in two regimes and two library versions); offer grid-shift alignment as an opt-in; retire the taper when the formula lands. Doing nothing is not defensible here — the shipped default visibly stripes at 8× downsampling.

1  Axial truncation (detector rows / z)

The problem and the remedy

When the object extends past the covered slab in z, the unexplained attenuation piles up at the slab ends as a bright flash with z-ringing that grows with iterations (Phase 1: end NRMSE 9× worse than padded). The remedy is reconstruction support, not data manipulation: extend the slab so every measured ray is explainable. As seen in the figure below, the maximum axial extension is bounded by geometry. A detector point at height v (relative to the plane of the source) sees material out to z = v·(SID + R)/SDD at the far side of the support, so the extension is computed per end, as the excess of each detector edge's reach over the slab the existing calculation already provides:

Etop = max(0,  vtop·(SID + R)/SDD − Hiso/2),   Ebot = max(0,  |vbot|·(SID + R)/SDD − Hiso/2),

vtop = Nrows·δrow/2 − det_row_offset,   vbot = −Nrows·δ row/2 − det_row_offset,   R = ½·max(Nr·δvox,r, Nc·δvox,c).

Sign convention: these are the library's own (detector_mn_to_uv: v = (m − center)·δrow − det_row_offset), in which a negative offset shifts the detector up — e.g. the NSI Lilly scan's det_row_offset of −1.95 rows pairs with a recon_slice_offset of +1.95 slices. An earlier revision of this page wrote vtop with the opposite offset sign; the implementation calls detector_mn_to_uv directly so the code cannot drift from the convention.

Three things to notice. The two ends differ: a nonzero det_row_offset makes one detector edge taller than the other, so its wedge is deeper — translate the detector up and the top wedge grows while the bottom one shrinks (figure below) — and a single symmetric scale factor would under-pad one end by exactly the offset term. R is the radius of the reconstruction support, from the recon shape and voxel widths, not from the sinogram: the bound is set by which voxels rays can traverse, so it must track the actual grid (for the default shape this reduces to the familiar R/SID = C·δc/(2·SDD) ≈ 0.1–0.2, i.e. a ~10–20% total extension). And helical z-shifts attach naturally per end: the top excess extends the coverage that already ends at zmax of the travel, the bottom excess the end at zmin; the interior of the travel is already covered. Two facts make this bound special (Phase 2a): padding past it is bit-exact identical to padding to it (the extra slices intersect no rays), so there is no over-padding risk to tune away; and the row taper is strictly counterproductive here (2.4× worse alone; hurts every padded variant it was added to), so the remedy has one moving part.

Recommended implementation

Do the extension automatically where the recon shape is born — ConeBeamModel.auto_set_recon_geometry — which today sets num_recon_slices = ceil((H_iso + z_travel) / δ_slice) centered at mid-travel. The per-end excesses are added to that existing calculation, and the formulas are not new: they are the model's own coordinate chain (recon_ijk_to_xyz → geometry_xyz_to_uv_mag, where pixel_mag = 1/(1/mag − y/SDD), → detector_uv_to_mn) inverted at the detector row edges and evaluated at the far side of the support (y = −R, where pixel_mag is smallest and z = v·(SID+R)/SDD is largest):

# in ConeBeamModel.auto_set_recon_geometry, after the existing
# num_recon_slices = ceil((H_iso + z_travel) / delta_voxel_slice):
R = 0.5 * max(num_recon_rows * delta_voxel_row, num_recon_cols * delta_voxel)  # support radius (note below)

# detector row EDGES in v: invert detector_uv_to_mn at m = -0.5 and num_det_rows - 0.5
v_top = (num_det_rows - 0.5 - det_center_row) * delta_det_row - det_row_offset
v_bot = (-0.5 - det_center_row) * delta_det_row - det_row_offset

# reach at the far side of the support (y = -R); excess over the existing symmetric slab
excess_top = max(0.0, v_top * (SID + R) / SDD - H_iso / 2)   # attaches at z_max of the travel
excess_bot = max(0.0, -v_bot * (SID + R) / SDD - H_iso / 2)  # attaches at z_min

n_top = int(jnp.ceil(excess_top / delta_voxel_slice))
n_bot = int(jnp.ceil(excess_bot / delta_voxel_slice))
num_recon_slices += n_top + n_bot
recon_slice_offset += 0.5 * (n_top - n_bot) * delta_voxel_slice   # recenter to the true reach

# in scale_recon_shape (which stays a pure, fixed scaler): warn on uncompensated lateral growth
if max(row_scale, col_scale) > 1 and slice_scale == 1:
    warn("lateral scaling enlarges the axial visibility bound; consider a compensating "
         "slice_scale (the per-end excess grows in proportion to the support radius R)")

Notes for implementation time. What exactly is R: the z-reach of a voxel depends on its distance from the source, which maxes at SID + (its distance from the axis) — so R should be the maximum axis-distance of any voxel the projector actually updates: the RoR cylinder radius if the reconstruction is masked to one, but the grid half-diagonal (√2 larger) if corner voxels are updated too; check against the projector, don't guess. Resolved 2026-07-11 from the code: both the VCD pixel partitions and the full-index forward/back projections restrict to get_2d_ror_mask, which is by default the ellipse inscribed in the grid — so R is the voxel-edge half-width of the longer axis (the formula above), and the implementation reads the use_ror_mask parameter: False or a custom mask updates corner voxels and switches R to the grid half-diagonal. Side benefit: because the two ends are computed separately, the automatic shape becomes correct for offset detectors — today's calculation is det_row_offset-blind and preprocessing pipelines compensate by hand-setting recon_slice_offset. det_channel_offset: the z formulas are unchanged — an oblique ray to lateral position u has in-plane length √(SDD²+u²) > SDD and thus a smaller z-slope, so the extremum is always the near-midline far-side ray and dividing by SDD is conservative for the rest. A channel offset acts indirectly: over a full rotation it enlarges the effective FoV to (W/2 + |coff|)/mag (the offset-detector half-fan trick), which should enlarge the recon rows/cols and hence R — and flows into the axial bound automatically because R comes from the recon shape. (It also exposes that today's lateral auto-shape is channel-offset-blind in the same way — the same per-end idea applies to the channel direction; a sibling gap, out of scope here.) Large det_row_offset: if the offset exceeds half the detector height, both edges have the same sign — no measured ray reaches z = 0 — and the max(0, ·) clamps handle it with no modification: the near end simply gets no extension (the clamp in fact activates once the offset exceeds ≈(H/2)·(R/SID)/(1+R/SID), i.e. ~10–20% of the half-height). What the add-only design then costs is waste, not correctness: the offset-blind base slab keeps its unmeasured near end. The complete shape would also trim that end — to vnear·(SID−R)/SDD, the one place the near-side factor appears — a refinement worth doing only if half-cone scans matter. Escape hatch: users who set recon_shape explicitly bypass auto-geometry entirely, and that should stay. Scope: translation and multiaxis-parallel geometries have analogous per-end bounds; cone beam first.

Pros, cons, and the verdict

  • The pad size needs no tuning: it is an exact geometric bound, and exceeding it is provably free — the failure mode "picked the wrong pad" does not exist. Always-on also means no threshold anywhere: no detector, no air floor, no trigger to calibrate.
  • Truncation is the norm, not the exception: in practice some holder or fixture virtually always extends past the field of view at one end, so the extension is used on at least one end of nearly every real scan — the true waste is bounded by the other end's share (~5–10% of slices), and the holder gets reconstructed instead of flashing into the object.
  • Large, regime-robust benefit when truncation is present: 9× end NRMSE, interior z-ringing removed; ranking held under wide cone, sharp regularization, and photon noise (P2a-R).
  • The per-end form makes the automatic shape correct for offset detectors and helical travel for free (excesses attach at zmax / zmin), and computing R from the recon grid keeps the bound tied to the actual support; scale_recon_shape stays a pure scaler and simply warns when lateral growth is left uncompensated in z.
  • Default recon shapes grow by ~R/SID slices (~10–20%) for everyone — memory and compute, though slices are the linear (cheap) axis, unlike lateral's O(scale²).
  • It is a default-behavior change: output shapes grow and interior values shift (for the better), so downstream baselines churn — including our own regression dashboards, which gate on values and memory. That wants a deliberate re-baseline and a release note, not a surprise.
  • The end wedge remains half-sampled with or without padding (the extreme slices are smooth in every variant) — padding removes the artifact, not the physics; worth one line in the docs.

Verdict: implement, automatically, in auto_set_recon_geometry. The holder reality makes always-on the right default and eliminates the last knob; validate on the real SiC scan (Phase 3) and re-baseline the regression suite in the same change.

2  Lateral truncation (detector channels / FoV)

The problem and why there is no formula

When the object extends past the field of view laterally, every channel is contaminated (a point at radius r crosses all channels |c| ≤ r at some view), so the damage is not confined to the rim: a bright RoR-edge ring (~4.5× body) plus a whole-interior positive bias (Phase 1). The channel taper is falsified for this case — it cosmetically softens the rim and leaves the interior bias untouched (Phase 2b) — and the remedy is again support: pad the reconstruction until it covers the object. The knee is exactly at cover in every overshoot and regime tested, and the penalty is asymmetric:

under-padding → orders of magnitude;   over-padding → percent-level (and O(scale²) memory).

The difficulty: "cover" is not derivable. There is no geometric bound (the only physical constraint is the rotation clearance Robj < min(SID, SDD−SID), about 8× the FoV at our base geometry — useless as a pad size), and the object's support edge is off the detector, so truncated data does not show where the object ends. Candidate estimators all have real failure modes: edge-channel magnitude extrapolation is object-dependent and can under-pad (the catastrophic side of the asymmetry); iterative pad-recon-check converges but multiplies reconstruction cost; only the user's physical knowledge of the object diameter is reliable. A further expectation-setter from the 4×-overshoot showcase: with severe truncation, even correct cover recovers structure but leaves a near-uniform DC offset (interior-tomography ambiguity) that no padding can remove without a known-air anchor.

Recommended implementation

No new machinery is needed for the detection: auto_set_regularization_params already computes a sinogram support indicator on a view-subsampled sinogram (_get_sino_indicator, built on estimate_background_cluster_boundaries) as input to auto_set_sigma_y — a calibrated object-vs-air split with a built-in safety margin. Truncation detection is then just "does the support reach the detector edge," computed where the indicator already exists, at no extra cost and with no new threshold:

# in auto_set_regularization_params, where the subsampled sinogram's support
# indicator is already computed for auto_set_sigma_y:
sino_indicator = self._get_sino_indicator(small_sinogram)

edge_frac = np.mean(sino_indicator[:, :, 0] | sino_indicator[:, :, -1])
if edge_frac > 0.02:   # robustness guard against stray pixels, not a calibrated knob
    warn(f"lateral FoV truncation detected (object support reaches the detector edge "
         f"in {edge_frac:.0%} of sampled view-rows): expect an RoR-edge ring and "
         "interior bias; pad with scale_recon_shape(s, s), s >= object_diameter / "
         "FoV_diameter. Round s UP: under-padding is far worse than over-padding. "
         "Severe truncation also leaves a DC offset no padding can remove.")

The same indicator applied to the first/last detector rows gives an axial-truncation check for free, should one ever be wanted alongside the automatic §1 extension (e.g. to report how much of the extension a scan actually uses). One honest limitation inherited from the indicator: its safety margin means very faint edge attenuation can go undetected — the benign direction (mild artifact, status quo), and the same conservatism the regularization estimate itself relies on.

Pros, cons, and the verdict

  • The warning is free and threshold-free: it reuses the calibrated object-vs-air support indicator that auto_set_regularization_params already computes for auto_set_sigma_y, on the already-subsampled sinogram. No catastrophic branch, and the message names the fix with its one crucial usage rule (round up).
  • Automatic padding would need a cover estimate from data that cannot show the cover; a wrong guess either hits the orders-of-magnitude under-pad penalty or buys O(scale²) memory nobody asked for. Many special cases, one finicky threshold chained to another.
  • Assisted-manual leaves users who ignore warnings with the status quo — accepted; the artifact is visible and the message tells them exactly what to do.

Verdict: detect and warn; deliberately do nothing automatic. This is the case where the balance tips against automation: the estimate that automation needs is the one quantity the data cannot supply. Revisit only if Phase 3 (z62, BGA) turns up a cover estimator that survives real scans.

3  The split_sino_recon seam

The problem and the two-part cause

The split reconstructs detector-row halves separately and stitches; its seam stripes (±40% zigzag on real data) come from geometry alone (Phase 2c): each half's kept rows see hsino·(1+R/SID)·ρ slices past the split (ρ = pitch ratio ≈ 1) while its recon extends only hrecon — and the load-bearing driver is the sub-row misalignment ε between the sinogram cut row and the recon split slice (ε ≈ 0.4 rows on Lilly), without which the truncation is benign at the default depth. The shipped sine taper suppresses the stripes at 4× downsampling but fails at 8×; the shipped default is therefore visibly broken in a regime users actually run, and "do nothing" loses on the evidence.

Fix A (recommended default): the geometry-derived extension

hrecon = ⌈hsino·(1+R/SID)·ρ⌉ + m,   ρ = δrow / (mag·δslice),   m = 2,

so every slice the kept rows can see is representable. The conversion ρ is written directly — the physical content is the perpendicular-ray row→iso conversion 1/mag, and dividing by δslice puts it in slice units; at the defaults (δslice = δc/mag, square detector pixels) the magnifications cancel and ρ = 1 (Lilly: 0.9995). R is the recon-support radius, exactly as in §1. The margin m covers the voxel footprint and the worst-case half-slice cut–split misalignment — the seam-scale analogue of §1's per-end offset term. This is §1's wedge evaluated at the perpendicular ray, which is why the extension is so small: the seam sits at the iso row, where the cone divergence is minimal, so the excess beyond hsino·ρ is only hsino·ρ·(R/SID) ≈ 1 slice — in contrast to the slab ends, where the same geometry scales with the full detector half-height. Evidence: at 8× it takes the seam from 7.9×10−3 to 9.0×10−4 with the formula value (hrecon = 9), and depth beyond the formula changes nothing (h = 12 identical); at 4× it beats the taper and keeps converging (3.2×10−4 at 60 iterations) where the no-taper default is frozen. Threshold-free, regime-robust, verified on two library versions.

# in split_sino_recon, replacing the pitch-ratio-only overlap computation:
R = 0.5 * max(num_recon_rows * delta_voxel_row, num_recon_cols * delta_voxel)  # as in section 1
rho = delta_det_row / (magnification * delta_voxel_slice)  # slices seen per overlap row; 1 at defaults

half_overlap_sino = half_overlap
half_overlap_recon = int(np.ceil(half_overlap * (1 + R / source_iso_dist) * rho)) + 2

Cost at the Lilly 8× problem: each half grows by 4 slices on ~120, about +3% memory — small against the 2× the split exists to save. The current code's h_recon = h_sino symmetry is exactly the shortfall the stripes live in.

Fix B (opt-in): align the cut and the split

Removing the driver ε outright took the ablation seam to 1.1×10−5 at the default depth — the best number any variant achieved. But there is an honest catch: with ρ = 1 (the default), the row and slice grids are commensurate, so ε is invariant under every choice of cut row and split slice — integer moves change nothing modulo one slice. The only way to align is to shift the global reconstruction grid itself:

# opt-in flag: align_split_grid=True
eps = frac(split_slice_position - cut_row_plane_position)   # in slices, |eps| <= 0.5

ct_model.set_params(recon_slice_offset=recon_slice_offset - eps * delta_voxel_slice)

The shifted grid samples the same object at z-positions up to half a slice away from what recon() would use — an equally valid reconstruction, but no longer bit-comparable or registration-identical to an unsplit run. That is fine for a memory-constrained final reconstruction and wrong for A/B comparisons, so it must stay opt-in. (When ρ ≠ 1 the grids are incommensurate and a cheap index search can reduce ε without any grid shift; worth implementing as the first branch.)

The taper, and the verdict

With Fix A in place the taper is unnecessary in every regime tested, and Phase 2c showed it is a regime-dependent suppressor rather than a fix (11× at 4×, 1.3× at 8×). Retiring it alongside Fix A is the clean move; retaining it as insurance for untested regimes (helical-adjacent geometries, extreme pitch ratios) is defensible and costs only an invisible ~10−3 perturbation. Recommend: ship Fix A as the default, retire the taper in the same change after a Phase 3 spot-check on one more real scan, offer Fix B behind align_split_grid. One standing caveat: all of this assumes the circular orbit (source in the iso plane); any relaxation of that re-opens the design.

4  Cross-cutting principles

  • Support beats down-weighting. Every taper variant lost to giving the model the voxels to explain the data — at the slab ends, at the FoV rim, and at the seam.
  • Respect asymmetric penalties. Where a size must be chosen (lateral cover), under-shooting costs orders of magnitude and over-shooting costs percent: round up, and prefer exact bounds (axial, split) wherever geometry provides them.
  • Automate only where the failure mode is benign. The axial pad and the split extension have no catastrophic branch; the lateral cover estimate does — hence detect-and-warn there.
  • Validate on real scans before changing defaults (Phase 3: SiC axial, z62 radial, BGA severe truncation) — this campaign twice watched synthetic-only conclusions fail on real data.
