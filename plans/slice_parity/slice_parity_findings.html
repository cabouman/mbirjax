Slice-parity alternation: does splitting even/odd slices help cone-beam MBIR?

Contents: Overview · The idea and why it might work · Data and methods · Attribution: the null control · Parity is state-dependent · On real data: a quality option · Quality, not speed — and kernel-gated · Recommendation · Provenance

Overview and summary

MBIRJAX reconstructs by updating scattered in-plane subsets of voxel cylinders (VCD). Its per-block surrogate handles the prior exactly but ignores one coupling: the data term's axial (slice-to-slice) structure. In cone-beam geometry that coupling is what produces the "center-slice" noise near the fan plane. Slice parity is a candidate fix: split each update into even-slice and odd-slice phases (red–black Gauss–Seidel in z, layered on the existing in-plane subsets), so a slice's nearest axial neighbors are held fixed while it updates — decoupling the part of the data Hessian the surrogate gets wrong.

Bottom line. Parity behaves exactly as the mechanism predicts, and it does reduce error — but in the realistic operating range (sharpness ≤ 2.0, about 15 iterations) the reduction is only ~4–5%, while parity costs about 50% more cone projector work per iteration. Its benefit grows with sharpness, with iteration count, and with problem size.

Recommendation: keep parity as an optional quality knob for high-sharpness, quality-critical reconstructions — but it is not a change to the default schedule, and it only becomes cost-effective if a slice-set-aware cone forward kernel removes the extra cost.

The idea and why it might work

The companion diagnosis (center-slice preconditioner notes) established that the ATA point-spread function is only 3–5 slices wide in z. So within one parity class (say the even slices), slices z and z±2 barely interact through the data term: the block's axial data Hessian becomes nearly diagonal, which is exactly the approximation MBIRJAX's per-voxel surrogate already makes. Parity turns an approximation into a near-identity. The other parity class enters only as a fixed boundary condition (classic red–black Gauss–Seidel), and the prior loses nothing because its z-edges simply cross to fixed voxels.

Two honest caveats were flagged up front, to test rather than assume: (1) red–black Gauss–Seidel is a smoother, so it may not help the slow, low-spatial-frequency part of the error; and (2) the cone forward projector's cost scales with detector rows, not slices, so a stride-2 slice set still touches nearly every row — parity does not make the forward projection cheaper without a dedicated kernel. Both caveats turned out to matter, below.

Data and methods

  • Toy case. The center-slice-noise cone repro (128×40×128 cube, sharpness 3.0, noiseless) used for mechanism probes against a deep 300-iteration reference.
  • Real datasets. Three cone scans against 150-iteration references: lilly_ds8 (NSI D01788 autoinjector, recon 187×187×286), z62 (ORNL Versa cylinder, 512×512×640), and lilly_ds4 (the autoinjector at higher resolution, 374×374×667), each at sharpness 1.0 and 2.5.
  • Variants. "default" = the standard schedule [0,2,4,6,7]; "parity-all" = the same schedule with every iteration split into even/odd phases. Same seed, so the in-plane partitions match; parity is applied in a mask form that is bitwise-identical to the library solver when the mask is all-ones.
  • Metric. Cropped NRMSE vs the reference — relative RMS difference over the interior (0.85× radius disk, 10% of slices dropped at each axial end). Shown as a percent (lower = closer to converged) or, for differences, as log10.
  • Cost accounting (the honest part). Because the cone forward cost scales with detector rows, a P=2 parity iteration is charged 1.5 cost units vs 1.0 for an ordinary iteration (forward doubles, backprojection is unchanged). So 30 parity iterations cost 45 units. A slice-set-aware forward kernel would restore ~1.0; that kernel is the crux of whether parity is ever cost-effective.

Finding 1 — attribution: the null control is exactly null

In parallel-beam geometry the slices are already data-decoupled, so if parity works through the axial data coupling we hypothesized, it should do nothing there. It does nothing: final NRMSE 0.04217 (default) vs 0.04218 (parity) — identical to four digits. In the cone repro on the same phantom, parity changes the result substantially. The effect flows entirely through the cone data term's axial coupling, as the mechanism requires. This is the load-bearing control: it rules out the effect being an artifact of doing more sub-updates.

Finding 2 — parity is state-dependent (the smoother caveat, confirmed)

Whether parity helps depends on what error is left to fix, exactly as caveat (1) predicted:

NRMSE (lower is better). The two rows use different partition sequences, so compare default against parity within each row, not across rows — the coarse-to-fine sequence simply converges much further on this toy than the flat one does.

From a flat-fine start the dominant remaining error is low-spatial-frequency, where red–black Gauss–Seidel — a smoother — drags rather than helps: it anchors each slice to its fixed neighbors through the prior. Once a few coarse iterations have cleared the low-frequency error, parity helps at every granularity. The real-data runs below all use the standard coarse-to-fine sequence, i.e. the regime where parity is favorable.

Finding 3 — on real data, parity is a quality option that grows along three axes

Applied to the standard schedule on all three datasets, parity-all consistently reaches a lower NRMSE than the default at the same iteration count. How much depends systematically on three things: it grows with sharpness (a weaker prior leaves more of the axial-coupling error for parity to remove), with iteration count (the advantage accrues in the tail), and with problem size.

NRMSE vs the reference at 30 iterations, default vs parity-all (green = parity's error reduction). Absolute NRMSE is lowest on z62 (~2%) and highest on lilly_ds4 (~9–14%); within each pair, parity's reduction grows from ~7–10% at sharpness 1.0 to 24–35% at sharpness 2.5, largest on the biggest dataset. The same ordering holds earlier in the run but smaller — at 15 iterations the reductions are ~4–5% at sharpness 1.0 and 15–19% at sharpness 2.5 — so the advantage accrues with iterations.

The improvement is distributed across the interior, not a localized fix of the center slice: parity lowers the per-slice NRMSE at essentially every interior slice.

Per-slice NRMSE at the strongest operating point (lilly_ds4, sharpness 2.5, 30 iterations). Parity is lower nearly everywhere; the interior median NRMSE is 41% lower (9.0% vs 15.2%). The small bump near z≈330 in the default curve is the fan plane (the center-slice effect) — parity reduces it but does not remove it, consistent with the distributed-improvement picture rather than a single-slice cure.

Finding 4 — a quality gain, not a speed gain, and it is kernel-gated

Every number above compares parity and default at the same iteration count. But a parity iteration costs 1.5× as much today (Methods), so "wins at 30 iterations" means "wins after 45 cost units vs 30." Judged per unit of work, parity does not beat the default: to reach the default's 30-iteration quality it needs about 26–27 parity iterations at sharpness 1.0 and 16–21 at sharpness 2.5 — and at the 1.5× charge those cost more than the default's 30 units, not less.

Even if a slice-set-aware cone forward kernel removed the extra cost (charge → 1.0×), those same crossings translate to about 0.9× the default's cost at sharpness 1.0 and 0.5–0.7× at sharpness 2.5 — a real saving at high sharpness, but at sharpness 1.0 it still misses the 0.8× bar we set for replacing the default schedule. So the kernel's payoff would be "parity becomes a free quality upgrade at a fixed iteration budget," not "parity makes reconstruction faster."

Recommendation

  1. Do not add parity to the default schedule. In the realistic envelope its benefit is small and it costs 50% more per iteration.
  2. Keep it as an optional quality knob for high-sharpness, quality-critical reconstructions with a generous iteration budget, where it removes 20–35% of the remaining error — and note the benefit was still growing with problem size at the largest case tested, so the payoff at full production resolution is an open, promising question.
  3. Its cost-effectiveness is gated on the cone forward kernel. Parity is the concrete motivation for making the Pallas cone forward kernel accept an arbitrary slice-index set as a first-class argument; without it, parity always pays the row-bound forward penalty.
