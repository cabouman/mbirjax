Voxel-cylinder streaks under aggressive regularization: reproducing and measuring the artifact (Phase A)

The sharpness/snr_db schedule study, Phase A (2026-07-25, in progress), branch sharpness_schedule. Plan of record: plans/sharpness_schedule/sharpness_schedule_plan.md · scripts: plans/experiments/sharpness_schedule/ · raw outputs: gautschi /scratch/…/sharpness_schedule/.

TL;DR (Phase A, first pass)

At large sharpness and/or snr_db, cone-beam VCD reconstructions can develop streaks aligned with voxel cylinders — the vertical stacks of voxels that VCD updates as a unit. The working hypothesis is that the very first, coarsest random subsets take large under-regularized steps that plant cylinder-aligned errors the edge-preserving prior then protects. The remedy under study is a per-granularity regularization schedule: run the coarse early subsets at conservative regularization, reach the target values on the fine subsets, and leave the converged answer untouched.

Phase A asked: can we reproduce the artifact and measure it? Yes:

  • Reproduction, on the real scan. The BGA case reproduces the artifact immediately: the cylinder-organized error excess appears in the first iteration, and at full resolution it ends 5× more severe than at the downsampled scale, recovering only ×3.6 from its peak where the downsampled run recovered ×9 — at the default iteration count it remains at ~30% of its peak.
  • The first-subset effect. The streak footprint is enriched in the first-updated subsets (E(0) ≈ 1.4, decaying with update rank, recurring in each early iteration) — the hypothesis' fingerprint.
  • A validated measure. The severity metric — the axial power spectrum P(fz) of the fine-scale error, applied to a two-seed difference that needs no ground truth — ranks a known-worse configuration 8.8× worse, matching the eye.

Status: Phase A measurement is complete (downsampled + full resolution + metric validation). Next: pre-registered success criteria, then Phase B — the schedule evaluation.

1. The problem and the plan

MBIRJAX's VCD algorithm updates the reconstruction one random subset of voxel cylinders at a time — a cylinder being all the slices at one in-plane (x, y) location, updated together in a single step. The default schedule starts with coarse partitions (4 subsets, each holding a quarter of the cylinders) and refines toward 128-subset partitions. Two user knobs set the regularization: sharpness widens the prior (weaker smoothing, higher edge threshold) and snr_db raises the data weight. Both push toward under-regularized early steps.

On demanding real scans run with aggressive settings (the Zeiss BGA scan at full resolution, sharpness 1.5 / snr_db 35 is the reference case), reconstructions develop persistent streaks: individual cylinders, or clusters of them, sit visibly above or below their neighbors. The hypothesis, refined at the start of this study:

The coarse early subsets should make mostly low-frequency corrections. When the problem is under-regularized and the preconditioner is imperfect, a random subset instead injects high-frequency content: cylinders inside the first-updated subsets take large steps against the raw initial residual that their out-of-subset neighbors never receive. The edge-preserving qGGMRF prior then treats these introduced differences as edges and removes them only slowly.

The remedy this study is heading toward is deliberately simple: one (σx, σy) pair per granularity — conservative regularization on the coarse early subsets, target values on the fine ones. Because the objective is convex, the schedule cannot change the converged answer, only the path to it; and because each granularity keeps a single value, nothing about the public API or the compiled-kernel behavior changes.

Before evaluating any remedy, Phase A has to establish three things:

  • a reproduction of the artifact we can iterate on quickly;
  • a quantitative metric that agrees with what the eye sees;
  • an attribution — is the first-subset story actually true?

2. Quantifying the artifact

Streaks are hard to measure well: they form inside individual iterations, they sit on top of legitimate image content, and on real scans there is no ground truth to compare against. Everything in this study therefore rests on a reproducible, accurate quantification with three components, built once and reused by every experiment:

  • a way to observe the reconstruction one iteration at a time (§2a);
  • a way to separate the artifact from the image without a ground truth (§2b);
  • two numbers that capture the artifact's defining properties: its organization along the cylinder axis, P(fz) (§2c), and its association with the subset update order, E(r) (§2d).

2a. Seeing inside a reconstruction: the per-iteration driver

A segmented driver runs the production vcd_recon one iteration per call through the library's checkpoint interface, with no library changes: it can change regularization between iterations, snapshot the volume after any iteration, and record which subset was updated when. The essential property is that observing does not disturb: an automated equivalence gate confirms that a segmented run and a normal continuous run produce the same reconstruction (bit-for-bit on CPU; to float noise, 10-7 relative, on GPU). Every run in this study — including every baseline — goes through this driver. Unless a figure says otherwise, runs are the library default of 15 iterations with the early-stopping threshold set to 0, so every variant executes the identical iteration schedule. (Iterations are numbered from 0 throughout, so a 15-iteration run spans iterations 0–14 and “the final iteration” is iteration 14.)

2b. Separating artifact from image: the two-seed difference

Key idea: VCD draws its subsets from a random seed. Artifacts caused by the subset draw therefore land in different cylinders for different seeds — while the object, the measurement noise, and every systematic property of the reconstruction are seed-independent. So reconstruct twice, changing only the seed, and subtract:

$$ d = (x_A - x_B)\,/\,\sqrt{2}. $$

Everything seed-independent cancels. What remains is the partition-driven, seed-dependent error field, on the scale of a single run (the √2 accounts for the difference holding two independent copies of it). No ground truth, no reference bias — it works identically on synthetic and real scans.

Where a ground truth phantom or a converged reference exists, the same measurements can be made on e = x − xref as a secondary check; the two-seed field d is the primary throughout, because a reference-based comparison also contains legitimately recovered detail that grows with sharpness — exactly the axis under study.

2c. P(fz): how is the error organized along the cylinder?

A streak is error organized along a voxel cylinder — but not necessarily constant along it: it may span part of the height or vary slowly. The right question is not “is the error the same on every slice?” but “how fast does the error vary along z?” P(fz) answers it in three steps, applied to an error field e(x, y, z) — in practice the two-seed difference d:

  1. Keep only fine in-plane structure: \( h = e - G_\sigma * e \), a per-slice in-plane Gaussian high-pass (σ = 2 voxels). Streaks are narrow in-plane; smooth quality differences are removed here.
  2. Transform along the cylinder: for each (x, y), multiply h(x, y, ·) by a fixed Hann taper \( v(z) \) — an apodization over the central 70% of slices that fades to zero at the ends to limit end effects — and take the DFT along z, written \( F_z[h] \).
  3. Average over cylinders: $$ P(f_z) \;=\; \Bigl\langle\, \lvert F_z[h] \rvert^2 \,\Bigr\rangle_{(x,y)} \Big/ \sum_z v(z)^2 . $$ Dividing by the taper's sum of squares is the standard periodogram normalization; it sets the reference shape: for an error field with no organization along z (each slice's error independent of the next), the expected power is the same at every fz — a flat spectrum whose height is the per-voxel error variance. Any organization along the cylinder appears as excess above that flat baseline, at the frequencies matching its extent.

The spectrum P(fz) correlates with the type of error: a streak constant along the whole cylinder contributes at fz = 0; a streak spanning part of the cylinder contributes at low fz; error uncorrelated in z (slice-to-slice “speckle”) is flat. Two summaries are used throughout:

  • severity  \( S_{\text{low}} = \langle P(f_z) \rangle_{f_z \le 0.05\ \text{cycles/slice}} \) (low frequency / long streaks: structure coherent over ≳20 slices) — the study's primary number;
  • character  \( R_z = S_{\text{low}} \big/ \langle P(f_z) \rangle_{f_z \ge 0.25} \), where the high band (variation on ≲4-slice scales, well above any streak-like organization) estimates the z-uncorrelated floor. For error uncorrelated in z the spectrum is flat, so the low and high bands have the same mean and Rz ≈ 1; for cylinder-organized error the low band rides far above the floor and Rz ≫ 1. The band edges (0.05, 0.25) are reporting conventions rather than tuned thresholds — both summaries vary smoothly with them, and every comparison in this study uses the same edges.

A simpler precursor metric that scored only the z-constant component missed that case (it is retired to Appendix A); P(fz) subsumes it as the fz = 0 limit.

2d. E(r): is the streak footprint tied to the update order?

The driver knows which cylinders belong to which subset and the order the subsets were updated. That enables a direct test of the first-subset hypothesis. The per-run streak map reuses §2c's machinery at its fz = 0 extreme: with \( \bar e(x,y) = \langle e(x,y,z) \rangle_z \) the error averaged along each cylinder (central 70% of slices), define

$$ m(x,y) \;=\; \bigl\lvert\, \bar e - G_\sigma * \bar e \,\bigr\rvert (x,y) , $$

the magnitude of the same in-plane Gaussian high-pass (σ = 2 voxels) applied to the z-averaged error: m is large where a cylinder's average error differs from that of its in-plane neighbors. Within one iteration of M subsets, let Σ(r) denote the set of cylinders in the subset updated r-th. The enrichment

$$ E(r) \;=\; \langle m \rangle_{\Sigma(r)} \,\big/\, \langle m \rangle_{\text{all cylinders}} $$

asks whether streak energy sits where the update order says it should. E(r) = 1 at every rank means no association; E(0) > 1 means the first-updated subset carries more than its share. Because E compares regions within one map, it is insensitive to the map's overall scale — only the spatial association matters. One refinement: E is evaluated over the eroded interior of the reconstruction circle, because the band near the support boundary carries a large share of m's energy (~60% on the real scan) that is independent of the update order and would dilute the association (see the figure note below).

The footprint probe, illustrated on the real scan
{{FIG:footprint_illustration}}

3. What the measurements show

With the instruments in place, three questions about the artifact on the real scan (the BGA case at its registry settings, sharpness 1.5 / snr_db 35; downsampled scale unless noted): when does it form, whose footprint is it, and does it persist?

3a. It forms in the coarse phase

The first question is when the artifact appears and how it evolves. Three variants of the downsampled scan are tracked: the registry defaults (sharpness 1.5, snr_db 35), the same settings with the per-slice damping disabled, and sharpness 3.

3b. Its footprint follows the update order

The second question is where the streak energy lands: is it actually associated with the subsets that updated first, as the hypothesis requires? The figure below applies the enrichment E(r) of §2d — the streak energy of the r-th-updated subset's cylinders relative to the average cylinder, computed over the eroded interior — to the first three iterations of every variant. Iteration 0 has 4 subsets, iteration 1 has 16, and iteration 2 has 64, so the curves lengthen from panel to panel.

Footprint enrichment by update rank, iterations 0–2
{{FIG:a2_footprint}}

How to read it: E(r), averaged over seeds; the line at 1.0 is “no association”. What it shows: at iteration 0 the first-updated quarter of the cylinders carries ~40% more streak energy than average (E(0) ≈ 1.4), decaying monotonically with rank — and the same first-rank enrichment recurs within iterations 1 and 2 (early ranks 1.1–1.5, strongest with damping off). This is the pattern the hypothesized mechanism predicts: at the start of an iteration the data residual is at its largest, so the first subset's update — computed against that raw residual — takes the largest steps and inherits the largest share of the injected error, while each later subset works against a residual the earlier updates have already reduced, and plants less.

How large is the effect? E(0) ≈ 1.4 sits between two reference points: 1.0 would mean no association with the update order at all, while streak energy confined entirely to the first-updated quarter of the cylinders would give E(0) = 4 (and ≈0 at the later ranks). So the first-updated subset is preferentially loaded, but a substantial share of the streak energy lands in the later subsets of the coarse phase as well (E ≈ 0.7–1.0). On this one scan and scale, that suggests a remedy aimed only at the literal first subset would address at best part of the injection; a remedy that treats the whole coarse phase conservatively — the per-granularity schedule's shape — is consistent with what the footprint shows.

3c. At full resolution it is larger and heals far slower

The last question is whether the artifact persists at the resolution where it is actually reported. The same 15-iteration protocol was run on the full-resolution scan (601 views, 1532 channels; volume 1532×1532×968) for the default settings (3 seeds) and the damping-off variant (2 seeds). The answer is yes: at full resolution the artifact is both larger and slower-healing. At the final iteration, the full-resolution severity is 5× the final severity of the downsampled version. The healing is also much slower: from its peak, the full-resolution severity recovers only a factor of 3.6 over the run, where the downsampled run recovered a factor of 9 in the same budget. The gap opens just after the peak — over iterations 4 to 9 the downsampled severity falls 61% while the full-resolution severity falls only 44% — and the late-iteration decline rates are similar on this measure, so the gap persists. The practical consequence is that at the default iteration count, a full-resolution user still sees the artifact at about 30% of its peak level. The damping-off variant ends another 2.0× higher still, and its curve is nearly flat; as in §4a, most of its extra seed-dependent error is uncorrelated in z (Rz ≈ 5, versus ≈530 for the default).

A separate observation comes from comparing the two panels below, now on the same vertical scale. The two-seed component (left) accounts for only about 44% of the total cylinder-organized error measured against the reference (right). The remainder is seed-independent: systematic, data-driven error, entangled with the reference proxy bias. No partition-side remedy can touch that part — the schedule's target is the seed-dependent share.

4. Validating the measure

A metric is only as good as its agreement with the eye. P(fz) passes two independent checks — one positive (it ranks a known-worse configuration worse) and one negative (it reports nothing where nothing is wrong).

4a. It ranks a known-worse configuration worse

The first check asks whether the number agrees with the eye on a case where the right answer is already known. Cone-beam models ship with an empirical per-slice DC damping of the update direction, and field experience with it is unambiguous: disabling it makes streaking visibly worse. That makes it an ideal blind test — a configuration whose ranking the metric must reproduce, and which played no role in the metric's design. It passes: on the two-seed field, Slow ranks damping-off 8.8× worse than the default (0.0132 vs 0.0015). The spectra below show the full picture behind that single number.

4b. It reports nothing where nothing is wrong

A matched synthetic case — the same geometry class, a BGA-like ball-grid ground truth phantom, realistic transmission noise, production weights — shows no artifact anywhere on a 15-variant severity grid (sharpness 0–3, snr_db 25–45, damping on/off, noise on/off): every score sits at the z-uncorrelated floor, and over a 60-iteration tail the two-seed signal decays five and a half decades to effectively zero — the fingerprint of the convex objective's unique minimizer. The instruments do not hallucinate streaks. The null is also informative in its own right: whatever drives the real-scan artifact involves something the idealized case lacks. A hardening follow-up (Appendix B) resolved most of this suspect list directly: adding lateral field-of-view truncation switches the artifact on (×20 in two-seed Slow by itself), the larger cone angle amplifies it (×264 combined), and the streaking then persists at the standard protocol — while the remaining suspects (detector non-idealities, beam hardening) were not needed. Details, the probe table, and a side-by-side comparison with the real scan are in Appendix B.

5. Implications for the schedule, and what is running

Each measured fact maps directly onto the remedy's design:

  • Injection happens in the coarse phase (iterations 0–2) — exactly the iterations a per-granularity schedule runs at conservative regularization.
  • The footprint bias spans the whole coarse phase, so the schedule's granularity-keyed form — rather than a first-subset special case — is the right shape.
  • Under-regularization mainly slows healing (§3a), so reaching the target values only on the fine subsets attacks both ends: less is planted, and the fine phase inherits less to heal at full strength.
  • Convexity guarantees the converged answer is untouched: the schedule shapes the path, never the destination.

One thing remains before Phase B (the schedule evaluation) starts: pre-registered success criteria, set from the full-res severity numbers of §3c per the study's standard of proof — a simple, robust schedule that is at least as good as current behavior almost always. The natural primary criterion is the two-seed Slow trajectory (smaller peak, lower value at the default iteration count) at matched final image quality; exact thresholds to be agreed before the Phase B runs.

Appendix A — retired: the z-constant streak score

The study's first metric scored only the z-constant component of cylinder error: S = in-plane high-pass energy of the z-average of e, reported against a per-slice control (the z-uncorrelated remainder). It detected the artifact's birth on the real scan (S/control = 7.7 after iteration 0) but failed its validation case: with the cone-beam per-slice damping disabled — a configuration known to streak worse — S barely moved while the control exploded, because the extra cylinder error varies along z and the z-average cancels it. P(fz) (§2c) resolves the full range of z-organization and subsumes S as its fz = 0 limit.

Appendix B — the synthetic null, in detail

The synthetic control was built at 256 scale: cone-beam at magnification 2, a ball-grid ground truth phantom (a plastic-like slab holding a lattice of small dense spheres, mimicking the BGA's solder balls), Poisson transmission noise at I0 = 104, and the production transmission_root weights computed from the noiseless sinogram (so noise on/off comparisons hold the preconditioner fixed). The severity grid swept sharpness 0–3, snr_db 25–45, a balance-matched diagonal, damping on/off, noise on/off, and a 60-iteration long tail — fifteen variants in all, with 2–3 partition seeds each.

The figure below places the synthetic case next to the real scan it imitates — the projection data on top, reconstructions below. The likeness is deliberate: ball-grid contrast, transmission noise, the same geometry class. The differences are the null's suspect list (§4b): the synthetic detector is ideal, its cone angle and resolution are smaller, there is no beam hardening, the synthetic ball lattice is far sparser and coarser than the real package's (visible below) — and, notably, the synthetic object is fully contained in the field of view, while the real scan is laterally truncated (the source of the flash arcs in §2d).

Hardening the synthetic: what turns the artifact on

A follow-up asked which missing ingredient the null actually hinged on, by adding the suspects one at a time and probing each configuration for six iterations (two seeds; scored on the two-seed Slow and on a center-slice indicator). Two levers were tested: a larger cone angle (source distances shortened at fixed magnification: half-angle 7.1° → 11.3°) and genuine lateral truncation (a wider slab, 1.08× the field of view, forward-projected from an enlarged ground truth phantom onto the same 256-channel detector — the object really leaves the field of view, as in the real scan).
